Every book begins with a promise. The first sentence tells you what kind of attention the writer wants from you, and what you will get in return. Austen’s promises comedy, and she keeps it for three hundred pages.

## The trick in plain sight

Read the first sentence again. It sounds like a proverb: balanced, confident, impossible to argue with. Then it lands on “wife”, and you realise who actually believes this truth. Not the single man. The neighbours. The mothers. The whole sentence is said in someone else’s voice, and Austen lets you discover that on your own.

That is irony at its simplest: saying a thing in a voice you don’t share, and trusting the reader to hear the gap.

## What to notice while you copy

- The comma after “acknowledged”. Modern editors would cut it. Austen uses it as a pause before the punchline.
- “In possession of” where she could have written “with”. The phrase sounds like property law, which is the point.
- The second sentence turns the joke around: the man, not his fortune, becomes the property.

## Why copy it by hand

You will read this sentence in four seconds. Copying it takes a minute. In that minute your hand will slow down at the commas, and you will feel where the weight falls. That is the whole exercise, and you will repeat it for ten days.
